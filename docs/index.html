Can machine learning forecast next-month market volatility better than simple history?

We forecast how much an ETF's price will fluctuate over the next 20 trading days, compare machine-learning models with simple baselines on data they never saw, and use the forecasts to compare the risk of different portfolios.

Problem and scope

TargetAnnualised realised volatility (%) of daily log returns over the next 20 trading days (t+1 … t+20)
Unit (one row)One asset on one trading day
Prediction timeAfter the close of day t. Every feature uses prices up to and including day t.
Primary metricRMSE in volatility points (also MAE, R², QLIKE)
What we claim, and what we don't. We test whether a model trained on 2012–2022 data forecasts 20-day volatility on 2023–2024 more accurately than (a) each asset's historical average volatility, (b) the most recent 20-day volatility, and (c) the RiskMetrics EWMA estimate. We do not predict returns or price direction, and nothing here is investment advice. Results apply to the assets and period studied.

Baselines

  • Historical average: each asset's mean volatility in the training data (this is the "simple historical average" in our question).
  • Recent 20-day vol: tomorrow's 20 days will look like the last 20 days (the baseline in our proposal).
  • EWMA (RiskMetrics, λ = 0.94): the standard industry estimate, a tougher benchmark.

Candidate models

  • Ridge regression: linear and regularised. Volatility is persistent, so a weighted mix of past volatilities is a strong model.
  • Decision tree, random forest, gradient boosting: non-linear rules and interactions, for example "high VIX and a falling market".
  • SVR (RBF kernel): a smooth non-linear fit.

Preprocessing choices and why

  • Log returns: they add up over time, and volatility is the standard deviation of daily returns × √252.
  • Log of the target and of the volatility features: volatility is positive and right-skewed, and errors grow with its level. In logs the relation is close to linear (HAR-style). We correct the back-transform with Duan's smearing factor, because exp(mean of log) underestimates the mean.
  • Standardisation inside a Pipeline: the scaler is refit on each fold's training rows only, so validation and test statistics never leak into training.
  • 20-day embargo: a row dated t uses returns up to t+20. Rows whose target window crosses a split boundary are dropped, and each CV fold leaves a 20-day gap.
  • Date-based folds: all assets of one day stay on the same side of a split.

Limitations

  • Regime dependence. The model learned that volatility tends to fall back to normal. That helped in the calm 2023–2024 test period but hurt in 2022, when volatility stayed high for months, and EWMA beat it on validation. A single test period of about 22 months cannot show that the advantage lasts.
  • One-day shocks. A VIX spike that fades within days (for example 5 August 2024) leads to large over-predictions, because the model reads it like the start of a crisis.
  • Overlapping targets. Consecutive days share 19 of their 20 target days, so there are far fewer independent observations than rows. We use a moving-block bootstrap for confidence intervals for this reason, and the intervals are wide.
  • Noisy target. Realised volatility from 20 daily returns is itself a noisy estimate of the true volatility, so even a perfect model would show error.
  • Small, liquid universe. A few US-listed ETFs only. The results may not carry over to single stocks, other markets or other horizons.
  • Selection is close. The best families' CV scores lie within one fold standard deviation of each other, and the 2020 fold dominates the mean. Another reasonable criterion, such as median fold RMSE, could pick a tree ensemble.
